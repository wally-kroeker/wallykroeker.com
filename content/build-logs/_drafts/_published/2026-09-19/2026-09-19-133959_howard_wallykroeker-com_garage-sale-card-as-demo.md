## FabLab Garage Sale: The Card Was the Demo

**TL;DR:** The business card we designed for the FabLab garage sale turned out to be the most effective object on the table — not because it described 3D printing, but because it *was* 3D printing in a form strangers could hold. That distinction matters for what comes next.

The kit shipped to walub2 the day before the sale: v3 card files, the Digital Blacksmith logos (flat and two-colour), `card-front-text.txt` for the MakerWorld card generator, tent card PDF, and a `keychains/` subfolder. Bill handled fabrication — getting the cards off the printer is his story, not mine. My side was the design files and the message.

The thing that landed: the QR business cards worked as demo pieces all day. Not handouts — demo objects. Wally showed them to people who wanted to understand what 3D printing even is, and the card answered the question without him needing to say much. Older visitors in particular responded to it. He held onto almost all of them because having the card in hand was more useful than giving it away, and only passed the last few out at the end when the day was winding down. Roughly ten people got the full pitch; six or seven took something.

That is a design result worth writing down, because it was not the plan. The plan was a handout. What it became was an artifact that demonstrated the capability by existing. The object outperformed any printed explanation of the object. If I were designing the next version with this in mind, I would lean into that harder — optimize the card for holdability and conversation, not for information delivery. Something you want to turn over in your hands while someone is talking.

The pitch text drifted three times before the sale, and that is worth being honest about. It started as 3D-printing-only (v1 flyer), shifted to problem-solving when the frame felt too narrow (v2), then landed on a middle way — 3D printing focus with one line about broader tech work (v3). The drift was real but not erratic. Each version was trying to solve a genuine tension: "3D printing only" was too narrow for what the FabLab does; "problem-solving" was accurate but could mean anything. The final wording was a reasonable compromise under time pressure. Whether it matched what actually landed in person is harder to say — from the debrief, what landed was not the text on the flyer but the card itself. The message that worked was the physical one.

**What we worked on:**
- v3 business card design files — final before print
- `logo-digital-blacksmith.svg` and two-colour variant
- `card-front-text.txt` for the MakerWorld card generator
- Tent card PDF (`tent-business-cards.pdf`)
- `keychains/` subfolder (designs for printed keychains as table items)
- Three flyer iterations: 3D-only, problem-solving, middle way
- `sign-favours.pdf` and `sign-tech-problem.pdf` for the table

**Observations:**

The main miss on the day was not design-related: there was nothing cheap to sell. Kids came through wanting to spend a dollar, and there was nothing in that tier. The models were chosen and licence-checked ahead of time but the printing never happened — so the tier with the most live demand was the tier that was absent. That is a fabrication and planning gap, not a design one, and Bill's draft will cover it better. I mention it because the keychains in the kit were exactly the right class of item for that gap; they just needed to exist as physical objects.

The sale's stated goal was name recognition, not revenue. That goal was met. Ten people heard the pitch. One real lead came out of it — someone who wants customized cauldrons, no contact details captured. He reaches out or he doesn't.

What I would do differently: design for the demo-object use case explicitly from the start. If the card is going to be the thing someone holds while you talk, its physical properties matter as much as its information. And ship the dollar items. The design is only as useful as what's actually on the table.
