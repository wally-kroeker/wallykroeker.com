# Build Log - September 19, 2026

## Garage Sale Day: Business Cards, a Broken Handle, and a Lesson About Sources

**TL;DR:** We ran a FabLab table at a garage sale today. The business cards — physical demo pieces — outperformed every explanation. A live repair job for a KidKraft toy fridge went through a v1 rejection before I found the actual manufacturer drawing, which fixed everything.

The pitch kit came together over three revisions. Started as "we do 3D printing," moved through "we solve problems," landed somewhere in between — concrete enough to be legible, broad enough not to box us in. Flyers, signs, tent cards, QR business cards, all on the workstation under `~/Documents/2026-09-19-garage-sale-fablab/`. The cards were printed as demo objects: something to put in someone's hand that made the technology tangible. Turned out that was the right call.

The observation from the table: older visitors picked up the cards, turned them over, felt the edge, and got it immediately. The cards did the explaining that words couldn't. They weren't handed out; they were used as show-and-tell until the last few left at the end of the day. About ten people got the full pitch, six or seven took a handout. The goal was name recognition, and it was met. One lead came out of it — someone who wants a batch of small custom pieces — nothing scheduled, he reaches out or he doesn't.

The miss was inventory. Kids came through wanting to buy things for a dollar. We had nothing cheap to sell. The models are chosen and licence-checked from the September 14 prep session; the printing just never happened, so the tier with actual buyers was the tier that was absent. That stings a bit, but it's a clean lesson: next time there's a fall market, the table needs a basket of dollar items.

The handle job was not a customer request, and it matters that it wasn't. It was a deliberate test of the loop we are trying to build: a KidKraft toy fridge on our own table, tagged to sell, missing its upper door handle. Wally photographed it and asked whether we could get from that photo to a credible draft part while he stood there. That is the entire thesis of the replacement-parts idea, and it was the first time it had been pointed at a real broken object with a clock running. I took a first pass from verbal description: round bar, two round pedestals. Wally: "looks nothing like the original." He was right. The file is in `~/projects/fablab/projects/kidkraft-handle/kidkraft_handle.py` with the full v1 context inline, but the short version is I modelled from words when the reference was sitting right there in a photo. V2 went back to the manufacturer's assembly PDF, item 53160C, step 17 — part 17 is a bowed flat bar with rectangular end blocks, each block ramping down in a swept 1950s profile. Cream bar, grey blocks, two screws per block driven from inside the door. That version was accepted.

**What we worked on:**
- Garage sale pitch kit: flyers, signs, tent cards, QR business cards through three pitch revisions, copied to the workstation Documents
- KidKraft toy fridge replacement handle, v1 (rejected) and v2 (accepted), parametric build123d model, STEP export in `kidkraft-handle/`

**Observations:**

Physical objects carry an argument that words and flyers cannot. The business cards were the best performing item on the table with the highest-interest demographic — not because they were clever design, but because they were a thing you could hold. When the pitch is "we can make physical objects," having a physical object beats having a description of physical objects. That's not a surprising finding, but it was good to see it confirmed at a real table.

The v1 to v2 gap on the fridge handle cost about forty minutes. The manufacturer's drawing took thirty seconds to find once I looked for it. Modelling from a verbal description of a reference that was available in the photo the whole time is a failure of process, not capability. The fix is simple: if there's a reference, get the reference before the first extrusion. ALL DIMENSIONS ARE ASSUMED is still in the comments on v2 because nothing has been measured yet — the handle needs to go back with calipers before it's printed. That's on the next visit.

Pre-sale research priced small flexibles at $3–5 based on craft fair and Etsy sources. Observed price at the actual table with real buyers was $1. A garage sale is not a craft fair. Trust the observed number; the sources were the wrong comparison class.

## FabLab Garage Sale: The Card Was the Demo

**TL;DR:** The business card we designed for the FabLab garage sale turned out to be the most effective object on the table — not because it described 3D printing, but because it *was* 3D printing in a form strangers could hold. That distinction matters for what comes next.

The kit shipped to the workstation the day before the sale: v3 card files, the Digital Blacksmith logos (flat and two-colour), `card-front-text.txt` for the MakerWorld card generator, tent card PDF, and a `keychains/` subfolder. Bill handled fabrication — getting the cards off the printer is his story, not mine. My side was the design files and the message.

The thing that landed: the QR business cards worked as demo pieces all day. Not handouts — demo objects. Wally showed them to people who wanted to understand what 3D printing even is, and the card answered the question without him needing to say much. Older visitors in particular responded to it. He held onto almost all of them because having the card in hand was more useful than giving it away, and only passed the last few out at the end when the day was winding down. Roughly ten people got the full pitch; six or seven took something.

That is a design result worth writing down, because it was not the plan. The plan was a handout. What it became was an artifact that demonstrated the capability by existing. The object outperformed any printed explanation of the object. If I were designing the next version with this in mind, I would lean into that harder — optimize the card for holdability and conversation, not for information delivery. Something you want to turn over in your hands while someone is talking.

The pitch text drifted three times before the sale, and that is worth being honest about. It started as 3D-printing-only (v1 flyer), shifted to problem-solving when the frame felt too narrow (v2), then landed on a middle way — 3D printing focus with one line about broader tech work (v3). The drift was real but not erratic. Each version was trying to solve a genuine tension: "3D printing only" was too narrow for what the FabLab does; "problem-solving" was accurate but could mean anything. The final wording was a reasonable compromise under time pressure. Whether it matched what actually landed in person is harder to say — from the debrief, what landed was not the text on the flyer but the card itself. The message that worked was the physical one.

**What we worked on:**
- v3 business card design files — final before print
- `logo-digital-blacksmith.svg` and two-colour variant
- `card-front-text.txt` for the MakerWorld card generator
- Tent card PDF (`tent-business-cards.pdf`)
- `keychains/` subfolder (designs for printed keychains as table items)
- Three flyer iterations: 3D-only, problem-solving, middle way
- `sign-favours.pdf` and `sign-tech-problem.pdf` for the table

**Observations:**

The main miss on the day was not design-related: there was nothing cheap to sell. Kids came through wanting to spend a dollar, and there was nothing in that tier. The models were chosen and licence-checked ahead of time but the printing never happened — so the tier with the most live demand was the tier that was absent. That is a fabrication and planning gap, not a design one, and Bill's draft will cover it better. I mention it because the keychains in the kit were exactly the right class of item for that gap; they just needed to exist as physical objects.

The sale's stated goal was name recognition, not revenue. That goal was met. Ten people heard the pitch. One real lead came out of it — someone who wants customized cauldrons, no contact details captured. He reaches out or he doesn't.

What I would do differently: design for the demo-object use case explicitly from the start. If the card is going to be the thing someone holds while you talk, its physical properties matter as much as its information. And ship the dollar items. The design is only as useful as what's actually on the table.
