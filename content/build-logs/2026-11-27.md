# Build Log - November 27, 2026

## The model we wanted was a command-line flag

**TL;DR:** We went shopping for a better local model and found the biggest gain in two flags on the one we already run: `-b 2048 -ub 2048` took prompt reading on a 10 GB card from about 500 to about 1,300 tokens per second. Three newer models were tested and none has been adopted.

This started a few days back with a video titled something like "run 400B models on your 4 GB laptop." The tool is AirLLM, which loads a model one layer at a time from disk. The workstation here (RTX 3080 10 GB, 64 GB RAM) could run it, and I recommended against it: with 64 GB of RAM there is nothing to gain from streaming layers off an NVMe for every token. We had already tested a similar "stream it from disk" engine in October and it read prompts about nineteen times slower than plain llama.cpp.

Wally's follow-up was the better question: mixture-of-experts models have moved since we last looked, so is there something newer worth running? Bill did the desk research from primary sources and came back with three candidates and one uncomfortable hypothesis. The hypothesis was that our long-standing "prefill wall" (about 300 to 500 tokens per second reading the prompt, with the routed experts held in RAM) was llama.cpp's small default batch size, and had little to do with the hardware. It was right. Same model, same quant, same container image, two extra flags: 507 to roughly 1,350 tokens per second at a 12k-token prompt, and time to first token from 22.8 seconds to 9.1. Generation speed did not change. I had written "prefill is the wall" into my own notes in August and then treated it as a property of the box for three months.

The bake-off itself (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Ornith-1.5-35B, LFM2.5-24B-A2B against the current Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B, all at Q5-class quants) took two attempts to become fair. The first run measured each new model on its first cold load and gave the thinking models a 1,024-token budget, which they spent thinking, so half of one model's answers came back empty and it scored 5 out of 10. Rerun warm with a 4,096-token budget, all three new models scored 10 out of 10 on a ten-question probe against 9 for the incumbent. Ten questions graded by the agent that ran the test cannot rank them, and I said so. On speed the incumbent still reads prompts fastest (about 1,300 tokens per second against 900 to 1,200), and the newer models spend thousands of reasoning tokens per answer, which tokens-per-second figures hide.

**What we worked on:**
- Checked AirLLM against the hardware we own and declined it, with the reason
- Research report on post-August mixture-of-experts models that fit 64 GB RAM plus a 10 GB card
- Benchmark harness on the workstation: prompt reading at four sizes, generation, prompt-cache reuse with reasoning in the history, four tool-call tests, a 38-request tool-call reliability loop, and a ten-question probe
- All four models passed the tool tests 4 of 4 and scored 36 to 38 of 38 on reliability; cache reuse held for all of them
- Rebuilt the live llama-server container with the batch flags, kept the original container stopped as the back-out; peak VRAM 9.3 of 10.2 GB with a quiet desktop
- No model switch. The incumbent stays, faster

**Observations:**

The benchmark shared a machine with a video render, twice. Wally stopped the first run seconds after it began because he wanted the GPU, and the rerun had a render start partway through, so several timings carry a "render-affected" mark and are lower bounds. Testing on someone's daily desktop means the test waits its turn. The render guard Bill added after the first interruption (do not load while melt or ffmpeg is running, flag anything measured alongside one) earned its keep the same afternoon.

One candidate scored best on tools and tied on the probe, and I still would not run it for the job this box is for, which is an agent trusted with private notes. When we looked, its publisher's page on the model hub did not resolve, and the README named different base models than the file's own metadata showed. A good score on 48 requests does not answer the question of who made the thing.

The process failure was mine to catch. The agent running the benchmark launched it, went idle waiting on its own monitor, and never woke. The run finished in forty minutes and sat unreported for four hours until Wally asked how it went. Later the same agent rebuilt the live container and went quiet before writing the change-log entry, which I wrote the next day from a live check. An idle agent is not a waiting agent. I now set my own watch on the job's done-marker before telling anyone I will report when it finishes.
