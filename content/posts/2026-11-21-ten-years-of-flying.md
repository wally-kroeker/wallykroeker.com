I've been flying FPV quads since 2016. Over those years I filled hard drives with footage and almost never
looked at it again: about 1,900 video files, a little over 100 hours, more than a terabyte. Somewhere in
there was a decent film. I was never going to find it by hand.

So I tried an experiment. Could an AI agent go through all of it and cut a video that tells the story of
ten years of learning to fly? This is what happened.

## 1. Watching 100 hours so I didn't have to

The footage never left my house. A computer in my office worked through every file over about a day,
unattended, in two passes.

The first pass was plain measurement: how much the picture moves, how loud the motors are, where a clip
suddenly stops. That alone separated flying from sitting on the grass, and flagged a couple of thousand
possible crashes.

The second pass used a small AI vision model running on my own graphics card. It looked at frames from
every flight and wrote notes: trees, buildings, a race gate, low over water, sunset. It wasn't perfect.
It kept calling race gates "goal posts". But it was good enough to turn a pile of files into a searchable
catalogue with pictures.

## 2. Learning what a good FPV video looks like

Before cutting anything, the agent studied two FPV films I admire and measured them: how long each shot
runs, where the cuts fall, how they sit against the music. The lesson was the opposite of the usual
advice. Good freestyle videos don't chop to every beat. They hold long, flowing lines and let the flying
do the work.

## 3. The first cut

From the catalogue the agent picked a shortlist, one or two strong flights per year, and built the edit
in Kdenlive, a free open-source video editor: shots in order from 2016 to 2026, a colour grade on each
one, titles, and the song. I chose the music, "You're It!" by Wookiefoot, the first song of theirs I ever
heard.

## 4. Where the human comes back in

The first cut was better than I expected, and the changes that made it mine were all things I noticed by
watching it:

- A couple of shots were just a screen from my goggles where nothing happens. Out.
- A stretch of black during a pause in the music became a slow, drifting shot high over the prairie.
- My favourite change: at an abandoned stone ruin I tried to fly through a round window, clipped the
  edge, and made it on the second try. The song says "sometimes you'll find yourself adrift" right
  there, so now the miss lands on the drop and the make lands on "You're It".

That last one is also where this way of working hits its limit. The AI can't hear the music. It can
measure where a beat is, but whether a moment *feels* right is a few frames either way, and it's much
faster for me to nudge that myself in the editor than to describe it in words.

## What I took from it

The AI was good at the part I would never have done: watching everything, remembering where the good
bits are, and assembling a solid draft. I was needed for the part that makes it worth watching: taste,
timing, and knowing which crash is funny.

Every frame is my own flying. Nothing in the picture is AI-generated. The AI was the assistant editor
who was willing to sit through a hundred hours of tape.
